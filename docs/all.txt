1. Self-Supervised Contrastive BERT Fine-tuning for Fusion-Based Reviewed-Item Retrieval
 Authors: Mohammad Mahdi Abdollah Pour, Parsa Farinneya, Armin Toroghi, Anton Korikov, Ali Pesaranghader, Touqir Sajed, Manasa Bharadwaj, Borislav Mavrin, Scott Sanner
 Published: ECIR 2023
 DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-28244-7_1 
 Summary: This paper introduces the Reviewed-Item Retrieval (RIR) task and curates the RIRD dataset (50 restaurants, ~29,000 reviews, 100 natural-language preference queries with human relevance judgments) that our project uses directly. It also compares sparse retrieval, Neural IR, and contrastive BERT fusion methods for ranking items based on review content, making it both our primary data source and the closest prior work to our proposed BM25-vs-embedding comparison.
2. Matrix Factorization Techniques for Recommender Systems
 Authors: Yehuda Koren, Robert Bell, Chris Volinsky
 Published: IEEE Computer, Vol. 42, No. 8 (2009)
 DOI: https://doi.org/10.1109/MC.2009.263 
 Summary: This is the canonical paper on collaborative filtering via matrix factorization, developed during the Netflix Prize competition. It represents the traditional rating-based recommendation paradigm that our project deliberately moves away from, since matrix factorization requires user-interaction history and cannot handle the cold-start scenario our system is designed for.
3. BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding

 Published: NAACL-HLT 2019
 DOI: https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/N19-1423 
 Summary: This paper introduces BERT, the transformer architecture underlying nearly all modern sentence-embedding models. It establishes the technical foundation for the Sentence-BERT model used in our proposed embedding-based retrieval approach.
4. Sentence-BERT: Sentence Embeddings using Siamese BERT-Networks
 Authors: Nils Reimers, Iryna Gurevych
 Published: EMNLP-IJCNLP 2019

 Summary: This paper introduces Sentence-BERT (SBERT), a modification of BERT that produces semantically meaningful sentence embeddings comparable via cosine similarity. Our proposed model directly uses this technique to encode both user preference queries and restaurant review sentences into a shared vector space for similarity-based ranking.

[8] The RIRD / Reviewed-Item Retrieval paper
Citation:
M. M. Abdollah Pour, P. Farinneya, A. Toroghi, A. Korikov, A. Pesaranghader, T. Sajed, M. Bharadwaj, B. Mavrin, and S. Sanner, "Self-supervised Contrastive BERT Fine-tuning for Fusion-based Reviewed-Item Retrieval," in *Advances in Information Retrieval (ECIR 2023)*, Lecture Notes in Computer Science, vol. 13980. Cham, Switzerland: Springer, 2023, pp. 3–17, doi: 10.1007/978-3-031-28244-7_1. (arXiv:2308.00762)

Summary (≈230 words):** This paper formalises the task of Reviewed-Item Retrieval (RIR): given a natural-language query and a set of items each described by many user reviews, the system must aggregate query–review similarity scores into a single item-level score and rank items. The authors note that Neural IR methods match queries to individual documents but do not natively handle the two-level item/review structure. They distinguish *early fusion* (average all of an item's reviews into one embedding, then score) from *late fusion* (score each review against the query, then aggregate the top-*K* scores). Because no labelled RIR dataset existed, they curated RIRD from Yelp: 50 non-franchise Toronto restaurants (minimum average rating 3, at least 400 reviews each), roughly 29,000 reviews, and 100 preference queries spanning five categories (indirect, negation, general, detailed, contradictory), with binary relevance judged by five annotators (kappa 0.528). They fine-tune BERT (bert-base-uncased, dot-product similarity) with self-supervised contrastive learning, exploiting item and rating metadata to select positive/negative samples. Their best late-fusion model (CLFR with same-item positives, in-batch negatives, and sentence sub-sampling) reached **R-Precision 0.545 and MAP 0.626 at K=10**, versus the best early-fusion configuration (R-Prec 0.438 / MAP 0.519), naive mean-pool early fusion (0.297 / 0.364), the strongest neural baseline CoCondenser (0.482 / 0.570), and BM25 (≈0.42). Off-the-shelf BERT (≈0.297) scored *below* BM25 and TF-IDF, showing fine-tuning is essential. The paper evaluates using **only Mean R-Precision and MAP** (with 90% confidence intervals) and aggregates **only via a top-K mean of scores** (K ∈ {1, 10, |D_i|}, with K=10 best); it does not test max or rating-weighted fusion and does not report nDCG or Recall.

**How it supports Ton's project:** This is the professor's recommended anchor paper and defines exactly the retrieval formulation, the RIRD dataset, and the five-category query protocol Ton adopts. Because RIR reports only R-Precision and MAP and tests only top-*K* mean aggregation, Ton's systematic comparison of alternative fusion functions (max vs. mean-of-top-*k* vs. rating-weighted sum), evaluated with nDCG@10, MAP, and Recall@10, fills a genuine, precisely-stated gap rather than a manufactured one. The paper also empirically establishes fine-tuning's importance and BM25 as a legitimate baseline, directly justifying Ton's baseline set (random, rating-only, BM25, embedding+mean-fusion).

[9] Explainable recommendation survey (framing the "explainable" contribution)

**Citation:**
Y. Zhang and X. Chen, "Explainable Recommendation: A Survey and New Perspectives," *Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval*, vol. 14, no. 1, pp. 1–101, 2020, doi: 10.1561/1500000066. (arXiv:1804.11192)

**Summary (≈210 words):** This survey is the standard reference on explainable recommendation. It organises the field along a two-dimensional taxonomy: the *information source / display style* of the explanation (relevant users or items, features, opinionated textual sentences, visual explanations, reasoning rules) and the *model* that produces it (matrix factorisation, topic modelling, graph models, deep learning, knowledge-graph and post-hoc methods). The authors position explainability within a "5W" framing (what, when, who, where, why) and argue that explanations improve system transparency, user trust, persuasiveness, effectiveness, and satisfaction, while also aiding developers in debugging. A recurring theme is that user reviews are a rich source of explanations, because individual review sentences can both justify a recommendation and surface the specific aspects a user cares about. The survey covers evaluation of explanations (online user studies and offline proxy measures) and highlights open problems, including the difficulty of measuring explanation quality and the tension between explanation fidelity and accuracy. It reviews explicit-factor and phrase-level sentiment models that tie recommendations to aspects extracted from review text.

**How it supports Ton's project:** Ton's explainable component—returning the specific review sentences that justify each restaurant's ranking—is precisely the "review-sentence-as-explanation" paradigm this survey catalogues, so citing it grounds the explainability claim in established theory rather than treating it as a bolt-on. Its discussion of aspect/feature-based explanation supports the optional flavor-feature layer, and its evaluation section motivates judging that layer against independent human labels rather than self-referentially.

[10] Conversational recommendation survey (natural-language preference + cold start)

**Citation:**
C. Gao, W. Lei, X. He, M. de Rijke, and T.-S. Chua, "Advances and Challenges in Conversational Recommender Systems: A Survey," *AI Open*, vol. 2, pp. 100–126, 2021, doi: 10.1016/j.aiopen.2021.06.002. (arXiv:2101.09459)

**Summary (≈200 words):** This widely cited survey reviews conversational recommender systems (CRS), which let users express and refine preferences in natural language rather than relying only on historical interaction logs. The authors argue that static recommenders struggle to answer two questions—what exactly a user likes and *why*—because they infer preferences passively from behavioural data. They structure the field around five challenges: (1) question-based preference elicitation, (2) multi-turn interaction strategies, (3) dialogue understanding and generation, (4) exploration–exploitation trade-offs, and (5) evaluation with user simulation. The survey stresses that natural-language interfaces create the opportunity to capture a user's current, context-specific intent directly, which is especially valuable for new users with no interaction history. It surveys methods spanning information retrieval, NLP, and human–computer interaction and provides a roadmap of open problems, including how to evaluate systems without large-scale logged interactions.

**How it supports Ton's project:** Ton's system takes a single free-text Thai preference query and returns recommendations—a single-turn instance of the natural-language preference elicitation this survey frames as the future of recommendation. The survey supplies the argument that expressing taste in natural language is superior to fixed category filters, and its explicit treatment of the cold-start setting (no interaction logs) directly supports Ton's motivation that content/review-based retrieval is the correct paradigm when click data is unavailable.

[11] Usage-related preference elicitation from review sentences

**Citation:**
I. Kostric, K. Balog, and F. Radlinski, "Generating Usage-related Questions for Preference Elicitation in Conversational Recommender Systems," *ACM Transactions on Recommender Systems*, vol. 2, no. 2, art. 12, pp. 1–24, 2024, doi: 10.1145/3629981.

**Summary (≈200 words):** This journal article (extending a RecSys 2021 paper, "Soliciting User Preferences in Conversational Recommender Systems via Usage-related Questions," pp. 724–729) tackles preference elicitation for users who lack domain expertise. Its key observation is that conventional systems ask users about item attributes, but ordinary users often cannot answer attribute questions—they know how or where they intend to use an item, not its technical specifications. The authors propose eliciting preferences through implicit, *usage-related* questions. Their two-step method first identifies sentences in a large review corpus that describe item usage, then uses a neural text-to-text model to transform those sentences into natural elicitation questions. They contribute a multi-stage crowdsourced annotation protocol for building high-quality training data and show, through automatic and human evaluation on a five-point Likert scale, that their neural models outperform a strong template baseline even with limited training data. The work demonstrates that review sentences are a mineable source of user-facing, preference-relevant language.

**How it supports Ton's project:** This paper backs two proposal arguments. First, it validates operating at the *review-sentence* level—the same granularity at which Ton retrieves and returns justifications. Second, its central premise—that non-expert users cannot articulate attributes but can express intent—is exactly the tourist/newcomer use case: a visitor cannot name a Thai dish but can type "something sour and spicy, not expensive." It provides scholarly support for why free-text taste queries beat attribute filters.

### [12] Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis benchmark (answers the M-ABSA alignment critique)

**Citation:**
M. Pontiki, D. Galanis, J. Pavlopoulos, H. Papageorgiou, I. Androutsopoulos, and S. Manandhar, "SemEval-2014 Task 4: Aspect Based Sentiment Analysis," in *Proc. 8th Int. Workshop on Semantic Evaluation (SemEval 2014)*, Dublin, Ireland, 2014, pp. 27–35, doi: 10.3115/v1/S14-2004.

**Summary (≈195 words):** This paper defines the benchmark task that established aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) as a mainstream problem. Using customer reviews of restaurants and laptops, it specifies four subtasks: aspect-term extraction, aspect-term polarity, aspect-category detection, and aspect-category polarity. For the restaurant domain it fixes a canonical aspect-category taxonomy—**food, service, price, ambience, and anecdotes/miscellaneous**—and supplies annotation guidelines with worked examples (e.g., "The restaurant was expensive, but the menu was great" carries negative *price* polarity and positive *food* polarity). The task attracted a large number of submissions and its datasets became the de facto ABSA benchmark, later extended in SemEval-2015 (Task 12) and SemEval-2016 (Task 5), and, more recently, translated into 21 languages including Thai by the M-ABSA resource. It demonstrates that opinions in review text are naturally organised around aspects and that overall sentence-level sentiment must be decomposed by aspect to be actionable.

**How it supports Ton's project:** This is the foundational paper whose aspect vocabulary the multilingual M-ABSA dataset (mentioned in Ton's proposal) inherits. Citing it lets Ton make his rebuttal to the professor's alignment concern precisely: the standard restaurant ABSA taxonomy is *food/service/price/ambience*, which contains **no intensity axes** such as spicy, sour, or sweet. That structural mismatch is exactly why the proposed flavor-feature layer cannot be validated against off-the-shelf ABSA aspects and must instead be judged against independent human labels—directly answering the professor's circularity critique.
